The Road Ahead

What I am working on? Thinking about? How am I feeling in 2026? How do we navigate these times? Will the university survive? What kind of a world will my daughter grow up in?! Many questions. I have no easy answers. The world, to be sure, remains on fire. In my courses, I introduce the notion of weltschmerz, a German word that literally means "world pain" and that translates to the following:

Weltschmerz: "The feeling of melancholy and sadness caused by the state of the world and the realization that reality can never live up to one's idealized expectations. It involves a sense of weariness or grief from the awareness of suffering and evil, often combined with a feeling of powerlessness to effect change." (from Google, perhaps ironically)

Perhaps a strange concept to introduce in, say, a computer music / design course, but I start with this definition to acknowledge the moment, to acknowledge that we all suffer, and that as humans we are often both perpetrator and victim. It is also to acknowledge what my students are dealing with (e.g., uncertain future, a cynical job market). "Back in my day..." I would say to them, "things were quite a bit easier." Welschmerz invites students to be vulnerable, to not discount the things that they don't always talk about, but carry with them. It is an invitation to be compassionate to others and, perhaps even more difficult, to be compassionate to oneself. It is an invitation to design tools more authentically—as one would make art.

My work has been an ever increasing connection between the tools we build and the lives we would want to live. I have advocated that tool-builders see and feel the pulse of the world, and to build with compassion; it reflects the belief that our work is intimately connected to our world, and that we cannot always "stay in our lane". The things that vex us, that we carry—concerns of environmental collapse, of wars, of the unquestioned use of artificial intelligence, of what America has become / is becoming, of the devaluation of education, to thinking about what kind of a world my daughter will grow up in—it is all part of the "human medium" in which we do our work. At this point, I cannot look away from the world even if I wanted to. It is part of the work. At the same time, so is hope, curiosity, and imagination. Writing Artful Design had only made these connections more legible and even more urgent to me. If we want to build for the world, we have to try to understand the world, and learn to feel for the world. As my grandmother said to me, "you learn to laugh with the world, and cry with the world."

The Work Continues
The ChucK programming language research and development is thriving. The laptop orchestra continues to serve as a laboratory, classroom, studio, and performance ensemble rolled up into one. We are currently taking a break from VR orchestra but continue to investigate VR as a medium for creative expression. I continue to design tools and to teach artful engineering. My students and I are instantiating a new artful game design research track and I will be piloting a "Musical Game Design" course, cross-listed between Music and Computer Science, next academic year. I will continue to question everything—starting with myself.

Yes, there will also be singing about the dark times."

Part of the path forward for me is look back. I am writing a new creative nonfiction book about my family and centered on my grandmother, who was born in 1919 in Beijing and who lived to be 103. Through great tumult, she never gave up hope, a quality I think she bestowed on her children and her grandson. As mentioned in my Professional Statement, neither Stanford nor my family has asked me to write a comic book about my grandmother, and I don't even know what the book has to do with music; but I am writing this book on the life of my grandmother and my family, interleaved with my lived experience as a Chinese-American immigrant and computer music professor. It is shaping up to be both a photocomic memoir as well as a “hidden” book on education, and a meditation on the question “how much of our lives do we get to design?” (as a spiritual follow-up to Artful Design, about how to shape our lives under conditions of uncertainty).

The wheel keeps turning. I am not optimistic about the future; but as long as we are here, hope must remain. Even as I feel the powerlessness as we head into uncertain futures, there are actionable things we can do. To keep making art, whatever forms it might take: tools, music, video games, etc. To insist on giving ourselves the tools of expression and imagination. To believe in the power of education. Whatever else lies on road ahead, this is the work as I see it. To put forth the best versions of ourselves, in order to stay alive, to stay artful, and to stay free.

As a marker of hope in trying times, I leave you with a reprise of Kelly Cochran's performance of the trumpet solo from "An American Elegy".
